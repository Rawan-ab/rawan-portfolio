Case study / Game design & development

One gathering.
Two ways to play.

Laffha is an Arabic quiz built for playing together. Groups can compete as teams around a shared screen, or each player can join and answer on their own phone. The experience changes with the room; so does the way points are earned.

01 / Context

Designed for a real gathering

A group game needs to explain itself while people are talking, laughing, and passing phones around. I wanted the next step to be visible: who plays, what the challenge is, how long is left, and what a correct answer earns.

That led to two ways into the same quiz: a shared team session and an individual room where everyone plays from their own phone. Both are available in the current game.

02 / The play modes

Choose how the room plays

The first decision is about the people and devices in the room. Each mode has its own rhythm and scoring.

01 / Team play

One shared game, rotating teams

The host chooses teams, categories and rounds. Teams take turns drawing a challenge. The host display shows the shared game while the teams use their phones as controllers.

Challenge value200 / 400 / 600 pts

A correct answer earns that challenge's points. Using the multiple-choice assist reduces the available points.

02 / Individual play

Every player has the question

Players create or join a room and answer on their own phones. The question goes to everyone, and each player submits their own answer before the result.

Correct answerFaster → more points

A correct answer earns between 600 and 200 points as time passes; an incorrect answer earns zero.

04 / My role

From idea to a working product

Built and iterated on the web project with coding assistance.

I shaped the game concept, the rules and the experience I wanted people to have. I worked through the interface and Arabic question content, reviewed the working build, and tested what happened during play. I then refined behavior when the flow was confusing or broke down.

The repository contains the frontend, question bank tooling, multiplayer logic, and QA checks. This was hands-on product and design work; I do not claim to have written every line of code personally.

05 / Design decisions

Keep the next move clear

Arabic-first play

The live game uses Arabic and right-to-left layouts so its controls and questions read naturally to players.

Two device patterns

Teams follow a shared host screen and use phone controllers. Individuals join a room and answer on their own phones.

Visible stakes

A timer and point value give the question urgency. Team scores stay visible, while individual results reflect both accuracy and speed.

Fairer team chances

The team point deck balances opportunities at 200, 400 and 600 points across the configured rounds.

06 / Iteration

Small bugs, big effect on play

A selected team answer could disappear when duplicate live updates redrew the phone screen. The controller now keeps the selection for that question and clears it when play advances. Another issue could send result feedback to the wrong team after a turn change; the result now carries the answering team's identity.

Those fixes matter because a lost choice or a misplaced result can make players doubt the score. The repository includes QA scenarios for answer persistence, feedback ownership, point distribution and mobile assistance. A final regression on separate physical phones is still recommended.

07 / Where it stands

Playable, and still evolving

The live browser game offers both team and individual play. Team sessions include categories, turns, timed questions, assistance and scoring. Individual rooms let players join from their phones, answer for themselves and see the standings.

I learned that multiplayer clarity depends on what happens between screens: whose turn it is, whether an answer was saved and why a score changed. Next, I want to keep improving question quality and test full sessions on separate devices. I am not claiming player counts or measured impact that the project has not documented.
